Background

How TF Architecture Studio came to be

TF Architecture Studio was incorporated in April 2025 by three longtime friends who had stayed in each other's lives long after high school — through architecture school, licensure exams, years apart working different markets, and the slow accumulation of a shared conviction: that the Philippine design industry had room for a firm that could do polished work and run like a real business.

They pooled their personal savings, registered the firm, and opened a modest office in Mandaluyong City — deliberately close to the Ortigas corridor and its concentration of developers, retail operators, and institutional clients. There was no external investor at the start. The capitalization was bootstrapped, the overhead kept deliberately lean, and the ambition quietly outsized.

A fourth partner joined shortly after. James Whitfield, an Australian architect based in Sydney, had been working informally with Rafael on project referrals for over a year before formalizing his participation as a shareholder and foreign investor. James cannot independently practice architecture in the Philippines, but he brought something the founding three could not replicate: a steady pipeline of outsourced documentation and production work from Australian firms looking for skilled, cost-competitive Philippine studios.

A full year into operations, the picture looked promising on paper. The studio had secured three local architectural commissions — two residential projects and a commercial retail fit-out, each valued at approximately ₱1.5 million — and two Australian outsourcing contracts worth AUD 500,000 and AUD 350,000 respectively, both coordinated through James. For a firm celebrating its first anniversary, the project load was encouraging.

The problem was that the firm itself was not.

Structural Problems

Cracks in the foundation

By April 2026 — exactly one year after the firm's founding — the founders had begun to notice that TF Architecture Studio's early momentum was masking structural problems that would not resolve themselves.

  • Two active projects had slipped five days behind schedule with no recovery plan in place.
  • Cash reserves were tighter than revenue figures suggested — profits continuously reinvested, no formal billing or collection schedule established.
  • The three founders disagreed, sometimes sharply, on how fast the firm should scale.
  • Marketing remained almost entirely referral-driven, with no deliberate strategy for building a public presence.
  • Job roles had begun to blur, creating confusion about accountability and gaps in follow-through.
  • Elena delos Reyes had developed a pattern of arriving late to internal meetings — delayed each morning by school drop-off three streets from the office. The tardiness itself was minor; the signal it sent about scheduling culture was not.
  • Governance questions had begun to surface: ownership structure, founder authorities, James Whitfield's legal standing as a foreign architect-investor, and Gemma's undefined participation in production work.

The founders had scheduled a strategic planning retreat. The agenda was expansion. The real agenda, whether they had named it yet or not, was survival — the kind of structured, deliberate reckoning that separates firms that scale cleanly from those that collapse under the weight of their own early success.

You have been engaged as an external management consulting team retained to support TF Architecture Studio ahead of its planned expansion.

Your task is to evaluate the firm's current condition — its financial position, organizational structure, legal standing, operational performance, and strategic direction — and to develop a set of prioritized recommendations that the founders can act on before committing to growth.

The founders are sharp, motivated, and not without ego. They will push back on recommendations they find impractical. Your advice needs to be grounded, specific, and defensible.

Final Executive Challenge

Present to the Board

Imagine you are presenting to the Board of Directors of TF Architecture Studio. You have to convince the founders — Cristina Tan, Marco Ferrer, Rafael Ocampo, and James Whitfield — that your proposed strategy is the best path toward sustainable growth, given a full pipeline, a cash-tight balance sheet, and a team already operating at capacity.

Overarching Question

How can TF Architecture Studio scale from a successful start-up into a financially sustainable, professionally governed, legally compliant, and competitive architecture practice — without compromising design quality, client trust, or long-term profitability?
